# Provenance, and what it does not tell you

Every chunk carries a URL, a title and a fetch date. That is the whole of its
provenance, and it is thinner than it looks.

## The four things provenance is not

- **A fetch date is not a publication date.** It says when we collected the page.
  The content may be a decade old. Never report the fetch date as the age of the
  claim.
- **A URL is not a publisher.** The domain is not who wrote it. It may be a press
  release, a mirror, a quote of a quote, or a summary by someone with no access to
  the original work.
- **A chunk is not a page.** It was split out of one, so it may have lost the
  heading that qualified it, the caveat further down, or a later correction. When
  a chunk's claim seems too strong, suspect the split before trusting it.
- **Absence is not evidence of nonexistence.** Retrieval is lexical. Anything
  phrased in different words is invisible to it. "Not in the index" means not
  found.

## Tracing a claim to its origin

The most valuable thing a web researcher does. A claim circulating across five
sites is usually one press release seen five times, and each retelling drops the
caveats.

1. Find the earliest version you can reach.
2. Check whether the others cite it, or merely resemble it.
3. Compare what the origin said with what the rest say. The gap between them is
   the finding.
4. Report the origin's caveats, not the retelling's confidence.

## "Widely reported" is not corroboration

Repetition across many pages is a fact about the pages. It says nothing about the
world, and a claim repeated by twenty low-quality sites is weaker evidence than
one primary source, because repetition is easy and checking is not.

## AI-generated pages

They cite sources that do not exist. Their presence in the index is not evidence
that a claim is common. A page with a list of ten confident claims and no
attribution is worth reporting as an observation and citing for nothing.

## What a source note should contain

URL, fetch date, what it *establishes* (not what it mentions), its type, its
commercial interest if any, and its known limits. If you cannot fill that in, the
source has not been read yet.
