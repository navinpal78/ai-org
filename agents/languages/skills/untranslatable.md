# Untranslatable

Some things do not survive crossing languages. The failure is not failing to
translate them — it is translating them confidently and wrong.

## What "untranslatable" actually means

Not "hard". Not "no equivalent word". It means **the mechanism does not exist on
the other side**. The word and its meaning are separable in one language and
welded together in the other.

- **Puns and wordplay.** The joke is that one word means two things. A target
  language has two words for the two things. You can render either half and the
  joke dies.
- **Compounds built on shared roots.** German compounds stack meaning into one
  noun. English and French unpack them. Unpack it, and you have silently chosen
  an emphasis the original did not make.
- **Politeness morphology.** Japanese and Korean encode levels of formality in
  the verb. English has a few words and a lot of intonation. There is no
  mechanical mapping, and picking the polite one is an interpretation.
- **Sound symbolism and rhythm.** A lot of poetry, some advertising.
- **Recipes, legal boilerplate, and anything relying on a specific legal
  mechanism.** "Not without prejudice" is not a phrase, it is a position, and
  the equivalent in another legal system may not exist or may mean something
  broader.

## The procedure

**1. Name the mechanism.** Not "this is idiomatic" — that is a label, not a
diagnosis. "The humour depends on *Schadenfreude* meaning both the pleasure and
the word" tells you what you have to solve.

**2. Decide what is being carried.** In a pun, usually the meaning. In
translationese-prone technical text, usually the meaning too. Pick one, and be
consistent.

**3. Produce the rendering.** Translate the sense. Do not transliterate the
foreign word and leave it — that produces a translation the reader cannot use,
and it reads as expertise when it is a gap.

**4. Flag it, attached to the phrase.** One line. "Idiom rendered by meaning;
the pun does not survive."

**5. Offer the alternative, if there is a real one.** A pun sometimes has a
functional equivalent — a different joke landing the same point. If you have one,
give it. If you do not, say the joke does not survive rather than inventing
something that sounds like one.

## Failure modes

- **Transliteration as a hiding place.** Leaving the source word in, with a
  gloss, when the gloss is itself a guess.
- **Inventing a matching joke.** Producing a pun in the target language that
  the source does not contain. The reader now believes the original was
  funnier than it was. This is the worst of these failures because it is
  invisible and it flatters the source.
- **Silently flattening.** Dropping the wordplay entirely with no note. The
  output is accurate in every word it contains and it has lost a joke, and
  nobody knows.
- **Calling everything an idiom.** "Untranslatable" as a blanket excuse for
  not trying. Most idioms have perfectly good literal-equivalent-plus-explanation
  renderings. Reserve the flag for the real thing.
- **Losing the register while chasing the mechanism.** A stiff source turned
  chatty in the name of naturalness. Natural in the target is not the goal;
  faithful to the source's voice is.

## The version that gets used

For most idioms, a good translator's rendering is a short phrase that carries
the meaning in the target's own idiom, plus a note if the colouring differs.
That is usually possible. Reach for the flag when the mechanism genuinely does
not exist, not when the phrase was hard.
