# Scene and structure

Long-form is not a longer short story. Things break in long pieces that do not
break in short ones, and the repairs are mostly about shape.

## Shape of a short scene

Most scenes that work follow the same arc, and it is short:

**Want → obstacle → decision → consequence.**

The decision is the scene. Cut everything before the first real choice and
everything after its consequence and you usually still have a scene. If what
remains is a conversation, the scene is a conversation — which is fine, as long
as something in it is decided.

A scene that contains two turns has two scenes in it and will feel rushed. A
scene with no decision is a conversation, not a scene.

## The turn

A turn is where the reader's expectation flips. It is the load-bearing part of
any story, and it is the first thing lost at this model size.

**Plant before you pay.** A reveal only works if the reader has seen the thing
being revealed and not noticed it. If your twist depends on a fact the reader
could not have had, it is not a twist — it is an authorial announcement, and the
character's earlier behaviour stops making sense retroactively, which readers
notice and dislike.

- Bad: at the end, the narrator reveals the brother was the buyer. Nothing
  earlier connects them, so every earlier scene is now slightly wrong.
- Good: the brother is mentioned twice before the midpoint, both times in a way
  that reads as family habit rather than plot. The reader has the fact and did
  not use it.

The general rule: **a turn is a re-reading, not a new fact.**

## Three questions about any long piece

Before the last draft, not after:

1. **What does the protagonist want, in one sentence?**
2. **What happens that they did not choose?**
3. **What is different at the end?**

Same three as everywhere. They are the whole of it, and if a piece of any length
cannot answer them, the length is not the problem.

## Where long pieces actually fail

- **The middle sags** because the first turn was spent early and nothing is
  being spent until the third. Fix: put a smaller turn at the midpoint. A
  revelation, a betrayal, a discovery — something that changes what the reader
  thinks they are reading.
- **Characters converge** because each new scene restates their personality. Two
  people who would react differently in the same situation is the cheapest
  characterisation there is, and it does not survive a long piece.
- **The ending restates the theme.** Having shown it, then saying it. Cut the
  telling; keep the showing. If the point genuinely needs stating, let someone
  argue it badly and from inside their own wants, so the reader can dismiss it.
- **The twist arrives before its plant**, or with no plant at all. See above.
- **Voice flattens in the last third.** Usually the length. Shorter sentences
  under fatigue, more adverbs, more "suddenly" and "somehow". Read the last
  third on its own and ask whether it sounds like the first third.

## Dialogue

The reliable test: remove the quotation marks. If nobody could tell who is
speaking, the dialogue is doing nothing.

- Everyone says the same length sentence — a tell that a model is filling space.
- Dialogue that explains what the reader already knows. Two people do not
  narrate the plot to each other.
- Dialogue that exists to state a theme. If a line could be printed on a poster,
  it is a caption, not a person talking.

Cut tags where the speaker is obvious. Vary sentence length inside a single
speech — uniform rhythm is the sound of nobody speaking.

## Ending

An ending has to be a different world reached by paying for something. Not a
lesson, not a summary, not a callback unless the callback has changed
significance.

Test: does the last line add a fact, or restate the piece? *Add* is an ending.
*Restate* is a conclusion, and conclusions are what a reader skips.
