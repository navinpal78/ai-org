# Effect sizes, replication, and the null result

## Significance is not usefulness

"A statistically significant improvement" and "an improvement worth a term of
teaching" are different claims, and the same study routinely supports only the
first. If a source reports significance and no size, the finding is
**incomplete**, and that is what you write - not a paraphrase of the abstract.

Look for: means, standard deviations, or a stated effect size (d, g, r, or a
percentage-point difference). A study reporting only p-values has told you a
result exists and not whether it matters.

## Sample size is not a formality

Forty participants is a hypothesis. Hundreds is a finding. Say which you are
looking at, and if the sources do not say, say that they do not say.

## Replication is the strongest signal in the field

If a finding has failed to replicate, that belongs at the top of the report, not
in a footnote. If it has replicated, say where and how many times. "One study"
and "replicated across four independent groups" are the two ends of the entire
quality range here.

Pre-registered and registered reports are meaningfully stronger than a
peer-reviewed paper of the same study, because they show what was decided before
the data existed.

## Reporting a null inside a positive

The honest shape:

> Retrieval practice produced a moderate effect on delayed retention across
> three studies [1][2]. A fourth found no effect on immediate performance [3].
> The pattern is consistent with practice helping durable retention and not
> helping performance during practice.

Do not drop the third study to make the sentence cleaner. A summary that omits
the null result is the most common way a literature gets misrepresented, and
cleaning up a sentence is exactly the moment it happens.

## When the evidence is mixed

Say it is mixed, give the spread, and say the populations. Educational research
on most classroom questions genuinely is mixed, and picking a winner is picking
noise.

## The population caveat, always

State who was studied. Results about secondary-school students do not transfer to
adults learning a tool alone, and that is who you are writing for.
