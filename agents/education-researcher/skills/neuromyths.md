# The myths, and the risk of repeating them

This field has a robust literature on which learning myths are false and a
persistent public belief in them. You will meet both.

## The standing list

| Claim | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|
| Learning styles (visual/auditory/kinesthetic) | No support. Matching teaching to a stated style does not improve learning. |
| Left-brain / right-brain learning styles | Not a thing. |
| Sharp critical periods for adult learning | Evidence does not support the hard window the idea implies. |
| 10,000 hours to mastery | The study said something different: time alone predicts little, and deliberate practice and early start matter more. |
| Growth mindset as a general booster | Effects concentrate in specific groups; the universal claim is weakly supported. |
| Learning by reading and re-reading | Weak. Retrieval practice is better supported. |
| Multitasking while studying | Consistently worse. |

## The trap in your own format

A myth written in order to be debunked reads *identically* to a claim when it is
quoted out of context. Your report is going to be read in fragments, by someone
scanning for the sentence about learning styles.

So write the correction unambiguously and never leave the myth standing alone:

- **Wrong:** "Learning styles are a common myth, along with 10,000 hours."
- **Right:** "There is no evidence that matching teaching to a stated learning
  style improves learning. Do not design around it."

If a myth must appear, the negation has to be in the same sentence as the myth,
and the sentence must still be correct if the surrounding paragraph is deleted.

## Do not overcorrect either

The debunking literature is sometimes as overstated as the myth. "Learning
styles have no effect" is correct; "therefore ignore how people prefer to learn"
does not follow, and is not what the research says.

## Legitimate use

Where a myth is directly load-bearing for a decision someone is about to make -
a curriculum, an agent design, a course - state what the evidence does support
instead, and say what *is* supported. That is more useful than the debunking and
it cannot be quoted as a myth.
